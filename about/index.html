About

What this is, how it is built, and where it stops

An independent record of grantmaking by 1,935 major U.S. private foundations, assembled from public IRS Form 990-PF filings. Every year published here reconciles to a total that the foundation itself filed with the IRS. This page states the rule, the method, the maintenance schedule and the limits — in that order, and without softening any of them.

Who it is for

Four groups get different things out of the same reconciled record. If you are in one of them, the rest of this page is the due-diligence answer you will be asked for.

Prospect research and advancement
Universities · hospitals · consultancies

Grant lists that exist only as scanned paper attachments never enter the e-file datasets that screening tools are built on. Those years are recovered here, page by page, and reconciled to the filing’s own totals — so a foundation that looks quiet in a commercial database can turn out to have a full record.

Foundation staff and boards
Program · communications · governance

Your own filed record, read back to you as a book instead of a PDF: every year, every grant, every purpose, tying to the line you signed. Useful for annual reporting, board packets, and seeing what peers in your field and your state actually funded.

Regional associations and philanthropic networks
Membership · field research

One comparable record across many members, cut by state, city, purpose and year. Because every figure ties to a filed total, a number taken from here can be put in front of a member without checking it twice.

Journalists, researchers and nonprofit leaders
Reporting · fundraising strategy

A sourced starting point rather than an answer: who funded work like yours, at what size, how recently, and where the record is thin. Every figure links back to the filing it came from, and the gaps are published alongside.

What it is

The Grants Ledger is a reconciled, page-per-foundation record of grantmaking by the largest U.S. private foundations. It is built entirely from Form 990-PF — the annual return every private foundation files with the Internal Revenue Service, which the IRS publishes for public inspection.

What it is not

It is not a census of American philanthropy. It is the top of the field by assets and nothing below it. The great majority of U.S. private foundations sit under the inclusion floor and are not here. Neither are public charities, community-foundation donor funds, donor-advised fund sponsors as grantmakers in their own right, or corporate giving that never passes through a private foundation. If a search returns nothing, that usually means the funder is outside the collection — not that nobody funded the work.

It is not an audit. It is a documented reconciliation of public filings, with the receipts attached. It is not an audit, an attestation, an examination or an opinion, and it is not legal, tax or financial advice.

It is not affiliated with any foundation shown. No foundation in this collection commissioned it, reviewed it, approved it, endorsed it, or paid for it. Every foundation appears because its return is a public document.

It is not a live feed. Each ledger holds the years that had been filed and posted at the time it was built or last refreshed. The refresh schedule is below.

The rule everything rests on

One sentence, applied to every foundation, every year, without exception.

The individual grants listed for a year must add up to a grant total that the foundation itself filed with the IRS for that year — to the dollar. If they do not, the year is not published.

The filed total used as the referee is normally Part I, line 25, column (d) of Form 990-PF — contributions, gifts and grants paid, on the disbursements-for-charitable-purposes column. 1,626 of the 1,935 ledgers tie every published year to that line exactly.

The other 309 reconcile instead to the grants schedule’s own filed total, Part XIV, line 3a, because in those returns the two filed numbers are not the same. That is a definitional difference inside the filing, not an error in it — in the cases worked through, a commitment recorded on one line against cash actually disbursed on the other. It is disclosed rather than averaged: each of those ledgers names its referee on its face and prints both figures, and the pattern is worked through with real numbers on the exceptions page.

Nothing is ever estimated to close a gap. A year whose rows do not reach its filed total is withheld and the difference is published. Every such year in the entire collection is listed by name on the exceptions page.

How it is built

The same seven steps run for every foundation in the collection. Nothing is hand-entered into a page; pages are generated from a frozen template, a per-foundation configuration and the parsed filings, so the whole collection can be rebuilt from source.

Fetch the filingsThe source is the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search public release of Form 990-PF e-file XML. Filings are pulled from the IRS’s own yearly indexes and archives, keyed by tax period and return type.
Parse the grants scheduleEvery grant row is read as filed: recipient name, address, purpose, amount, tax year. Nothing is normalized away at this stage — typos, truncations and run-together names are preserved exactly as the foundation wrote them.
Reconcile against the filed totalThe rows are summed and compared to the filed referee line, year by year. A year that ties is eligible to publish. A year that does not tie is withheld, and the difference is recorded for disclosure. This step can only pass or fail; it has no third outcome.
Recover what the e-file data does not carrySome foundations file a whole year as a single line — “See attached statement” — with the grants themselves in an attachment the IRS publishes only as a scanned image with no text layer. Those pages are read by optical character recognition and, where the software cannot render a figure legibly, by eye from the page image. Recovered years face the same referee as everything else, and are additionally checked against the attachment’s own printed total wherever one is printed. A figure that cannot be read is labeled unread rather than guessed.
Resolve recipient identityThree tiers, in order: one shared canonical form of the name; identity by EIN, where the as-filed name is matched against the IRS Business Master File and an EIN is assigned only when the filing’s own recipient state pins the name to exactly one organization or the name is nationally unique; and a short list of reviewed merges, each with a stated reason. Names that resolve to different EINs are never joined. Over-merging is treated as worse than under-merging.
Classify the purposeThree published tiers: keywords in the filed purpose text, then the recipient’s filed name, then the recipient’s own classification in the IRS Business Master File — the last accepted only when the match is unambiguous. Where nothing matches, the grant stays in “other” rather than receiving a guess. Every rule a page runs is printed on that page.
Check, then publishEach page is checked in a real browser before it ships: the reconciliation badge must read N of N years with a $0 difference, the year controls must match the corpus, the methodology section must carry the withheld notes, and the page must produce zero errors and no sideways scroll on a phone. A pre-deploy gate then checks that the published site actually matches the source files and that the page count matches the registry.
Why the check-before-publish step is written down

Every check in step seven exists because its absence once shipped a defect: a canonical URL served a months-old page while deploys reported success, and two sub-pages served stale copies with unrendered template placeholders. The gate is the correction. The update history records both.

How it is maintained

A scheduled job runs monthly, on the 2nd of each month. For every foundation in the collection it pulls the current IRS index, looks for filings not already held, fetches them, re-runs the reconciliation under the same referee rules, rebuilds the affected pages, regenerates the collection database and search indexes, and stamps every rebuilt page with the run date.

Two rules govern what that job is allowed to publish on its own:

Anything the standing rules cannot settle is not auto-published. It is written into the run report as needing review, and the existing page stands unchanged until a person looks at it.

An amended return never silently replaces a published year. When a foundation files an amended 990-PF for a year already in the collection, the refresh flags it for review rather than overwriting the record. Replacing a published figure without a human decision is exactly the failure this product exists to avoid.

When the IRS posts a genuinely new filing that reconciles cleanly under the standing rules, the ledger gains that year at the next run. When it posts one that does not reconcile, the year is withheld and appears on the exceptions page instead.

Stated plainly, because it matters for procurement

The monthly job is registered and enabled on the maintainer’s machine and has been run manually; its first fully automatic run falls on 2 September 2026. This is an independent project maintained by one person, not a hosted service with an operations team. There is no uptime guarantee and no service-level agreement — see support expectations.

What “withheld”, “excluded”, “bridged” and “recovered” mean

Four words do a lot of work across this site. Each has one meaning and only one, and every instance of all four is listed on the exceptions page.

Withheld
The grant rows for that year are internally consistent — they sum to the schedule’s own stated total — but that total then disagrees with the filed line the ledger reconciles to. The year is not shown, and the difference is published with both figures. It is never adjusted, prorated or estimated to fit.
Excluded
Not a disagreement between numbers but an absence of them. The filing reports the year as a single line and puts the grant list in an attachment that is not part of the public data, and no scanned copy carrying that attachment has been posted. There is nothing to check row by row, so the year is not shown as one. The dollar amount behind the missing line is still published, so the size of the gap is visible.
Bridged
The ledger reconciles to the grants schedule’s own filed total (Part XIV, line 3a) rather than Part I, line 25(d), because in that return the two filed numbers differ. The page names which line it used and prints both, along with the difference for every year. A bridge discloses a difference inside the filing; it never closes one.
Recovered
The year’s grants existed only inside a scanned paper attachment with no text layer, and were read back out of the page images — by OCR, and by eye where OCR could not render a figure. A recovered year is published only when it passes the same referee as an ordinary year, and it says on its face that it was recovered from scans.

Data limits, stated up front

Grants to named individuals never show the person’s name, anywhere on the site. Scholarship, fellowship, loan and hardship rows that name a person keep their amount, year, purpose and state exactly as filed — on the foundation’s own page, in search, in cross-foundation profiles, in every export and in the underlying data files — so every year still reconciles to the dollar; only the name and city are withheld: the row reads “Individual recipient (name withheld)” on the page, and the data files carry a placeholder in the name’s place. 33,682 rows across 318 foundations at the last build; the privacy page explains the rule and the removal path.

These are true of every page in the collection, always. They are here because a figure used without them can be wrong even though it reconciles.

Recipient identifiers require source review. Some parsed records contain a recipient-EIN field; its source should be checked before treating it as a filed identifier. The collection’s IRS-linked recipient records use inferred matches between filed names and IRS organization records. Matching does not independently confirm each grant or resolve every alias.
Identity coverage is measured for each release. The collection reports separate denominators for named-recipient rows, their dollars and recipient identities. Open the collection and choose Coverage for the current measurements. Both IRS-linked and canonical-name records can split aliases or combine same-named organizations. Review source names and addresses before relying on a cross-foundation total.
Recipient addresses and funder locations describe different things. Some rows have no recipient state; Coverage reports their current count. Recipient-location filters use the address as filed, which may differ from where the work happens. The main map groups foundations by their home location. Its state totals do not describe where grant-funded work took place.
Inclusion is by assets, and the floor is roughly $86 million. The collection is 1,904 of the 2,000 largest U.S. private foundations by assets — 95.2% of the IRS-indexed top 2,000, whose asset floor is $86,222,585 — plus a 29-foundation Western New York local slate reaching down to $10,304,811. The universe those are cut from is 127,326 U.S. 990-PF filers with posted filings. Of the 96 top-2,000 foundations not in the collection, 67 are excluded by rule, 7 are deferred, and 22 are still to be built.

Foundations are excluded only for stated reasons. A foundation inside the asset line is left out only on one of five published grounds: a foreign foundation whose U.S. filing covers U.S.-source activity only; a private operating foundation that runs its own programs and files zero grant rows; a corpus or feeder trust whose sole donee is its own affiliated foundation; a newly formed foundation with zero grants paid in every filed year; or a filer whose grants schedule is empty in every filing, leaving nothing for a referee to tie. Named exclusions and deferrals are listed on the exceptions page rather than being silently absent.
Not included: the foundations of banks, brokerages, asset managers and payment companies. This is a conflict-of-interest rule, not a data limit — the publisher works in financial services, so the corporate foundations of banks and bank holding companies, broker-dealers, asset managers, and card-network or payment companies are withheld from the collection regardless of asset rank. It applies whether or not the foundation would otherwise qualify by size. Insurers are not covered by this rule.
The years are not evenly covered, and the most recent year is thin. The collection spans tax years 2016 through 2025, but it is dense only in the middle. 2023 is the fullest year, present in 1,907 of 1,935 ledgers; 2022 is in 1,894 and 2024 in 1,842. 2025 is present in only 205 ledgers — 10.59% — because most 2025 returns have not yet been filed or posted. 1,801 of the 1,935 ledgers carry five consecutive years; the rest carry fewer because the foundation is young, filed nothing in a year, or has a year withheld. Never read a year-over-year line across the whole collection as a trend in American giving; it is a trend in what had been filed and posted.
Amounts are as filed, and filers’ own errors are preserved. No inflation adjustment, no restatement, no netting of refunds — negative rows are real and are carried through. Names truncated mid-word, two organizations run together with no separator, and typos in purpose fields all stand exactly as the foundation filed them, because correcting them silently would make the ledger easier to read and impossible to check against the source. Some foundations file most purposes as a single uninformative word; where the purpose names no field, the ledger reads the recipient’s own filed name instead and says so on the page.
Recipient identity fragments, and the collection measures how much. Because most recipients are matched by name, the same organization can appear under more than one identity. Measured on the 200 recipient identities with the largest dollar totals, 114 of the 179 real organizations among them (63.7%) carry at least one near-duplicate identity under a strict matching rule, and 132 of 179 (73.7%) under a broader one. A further 15.52% of all dollars sit on rows whose “recipient” is a pointer rather than an organization — “See attached”, “Various”, “Unitemized” and similar — where the filer put the detail somewhere the public copy does not carry.

Reporting a suspected error

Errors are the one thing this project cannot afford to be casual about, so there is a direct path for them. Email the address above with Correction in the subject and include: the page URL, the foundation and tax year, the figure you believe is wrong, and what you believe it should be — with the source, ideally the filing itself. Every report is checked against the original filing. If a figure cannot be reconciled, it is corrected or the year is withheld, and the change is written into the update history. Reports from a foundation about its own page go to the front of the queue.

Support expectations

Written honestly rather than aspirationally, because a promise this project cannot keep would undermine the only thing it sells.

One person, part-time. No service-level agreement exists. There is no support desk, no ticketing system, no phone number and no on-call rotation. Email is the only channel, and it reaches one person.
Corrections come before everything else. A credible report that a published figure is wrong is the highest-priority work item there is, ahead of new foundations and new features. The intent is to acknowledge a correction report within a few business days and to have it resolved or the year withheld shortly after. That is an intent, not a contractual commitment.
General questions are answered, on a slower clock. Questions about method, coverage, or whether a particular foundation can be added are welcome and are usually answered within about a week. Requests to build or recover a specific foundation are handled case by case, and the answer is sometimes that it cannot be done from public sources — in which case the reason is given.
Availability is whatever GitHub Pages provides. The site is static files served by GitHub Pages. There is no application server, no database server and no login, so there is very little that can break — but there is also no uptime commitment from this project, and no status page.
Nothing here is presented as a paid service today. The collection is public and free to read. No subscription is being sold from this site, no price is quoted on it, and no payment is collected anywhere on it.
