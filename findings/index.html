Money that names no recipient

4 findings

Some of the largest “private foundations” in the country distribute almost nothing that a grant database would call a grant. The ledger reports what the filing actually says rather than ranking these entities alongside grantmakers.

$14.6 billion
Novartis Patient Assistance Foundation

Each year's grants line is a single row reading “Various individuals” — medicines given free to patients. No recipient list exists anywhere public, by design and by privacy. The row is printed exactly as filed.

Filed, but itemized nowhere public

3 findings

A foundation reports a grants total on its return and points to an attachment. The attachment never reaches any public copy. The money is real and disclosed in aggregate; the recipients are not public at all. Each of these was proven page by page, not assumed.

$367 million
PNC Foundation

Every year says “see attached”, addressed to the foundation's own Pittsburgh grants office. Two scanned returns were inventoried page by page: 261 of 328 pages are the securities register, and where the grant list belongs there is verifiably blank paper.

J. Willard and Alice S. Marriott Foundation

Ninety pages across three filings, every page classified. The filings prove it themselves: the roll-up line equals the grants total and both columns of Part I line 25 exactly — which is only possible if not one grant was itemized anywhere.

The filing disagrees with itself

4 findings

The most interesting class. A filing carries two figures that cannot both be right — and only reconciling every row against a named total makes that visible. Nothing here is corrected; it is transcribed as filed and named.

$1,533,193
Bezos Family Foundation · 2023

The stock register's own book-value column sums $1,533,193 more than the filer's own printed book total. It touches none of the reconciled money — the grants themselves prove to the cent — but it is left exactly as filed and disclosed.

$5,363,749 over
Johnson & Johnson Foundation · 2022

The matching-gift register ties its filed sub-total to the cent, while the non-matching register itemizes $5.36 million more than the sub-total it belongs to. No subset of rows accounts for it. Publishing those rows would carry the year past the foundation's own filed total, so that section is withheld and preserved as evidence.

What the reading software got wrong

4 findings

Scanned filings have to be read by machine, and machines misread. These were caught because the rows had to equal a filed total — a database built without that requirement would have published them silently.

$2,500 read as $500
Hellman Foundation

The software kept only the tail of the number, and the result was right-aligned and correctly shaped — invisible to every check except looking for ink to the left of the digits. A second misread turned $1,500,000 into $500,000. All four scanned years reconcile exactly: 947 rows.

open the ledger ↗
$1,148,464 → $148,464
William R. Kenan Jr. Charitable Trust

A grant to the UNC Health Foundation lost its leading digit — exactly $1,000,000 gone. Caught by the position of the ink in a right-aligned column, then confirmed against the page. The referee is what made it findable.

open the ledger ↗
$26 on $43.5 million
Caterpillar Foundation · 2023

The rows summed $26 over the filed line. The cause is that year's register prints whole dollars against a total carrying cents — for 7,569 rows the expected drift is about $25. Rather than discard a verified register or quietly adjust rows, both figures are shown and the $26 is named.

open the ledger ↗
$2.3 million, self-inflicted
Herbert H. and Grace A. Dow Foundation

Our own reading tool inflated a year by $2.3 million: hand-read corrections were keyed by page and position, and page names repeat between filings, so one year's correction silently overwrote another's. Nothing looked wrong. Only the reconciliation caught it — which is the entire argument for reconciling.

Why any of this is findable

Most grant databases publish what a filing's data fields contain. This one requires that the rows add up to a number the foundation itself filed — Part I line 25, or the schedule's own printed total. That single rule is what surfaces everything above, and it is the reason a figure here can be trusted without taking anyone's word for it.

Transcribe, never correctA filer's typo, a returned grant printed as a negative, a line naming no recipient — all kept as filed and explained.
Publish short, withhold overWhen rows fall short of the filed total the difference is one labeled line. When they exceed it with no proven mechanism, that section is withheld — a negative grant would be a lie.
Never invent a recipientAn attachment reference is not a grantee and its address is not a city. Where the public record ends, the ledger says so.
Every exception is publishedBridges, withheld years and residuals all appear on the exceptions page — the collection's honesty ledger.
